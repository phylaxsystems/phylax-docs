Ethereum enforcement depends on participating builders, valid block markers, and complete guard coverage. This page defines those assumptions and the recovery tradeoffs.

## Enforcement Scope

- **Deterministic checks**: the same assertion, execution context, and state produce the same result.
- **Validation before inclusion**: the enforcer reports failures before inclusion; the enforcing integration excludes those candidate transactions.
- **Assertion-defined coverage**: rules protect only the behavior their logic and triggers capture. Passing a rule is not proof of safety against other failures.
- **Configured lifecycle**: the State Oracle determines assertion assignments and activation or removal times.
- **Execution-path coverage**: every protected Ethereum operation must reach the guard, regardless of its submitter or entry point.

Assertions do not replace ordinary chain validity, protocol checks, or finality rules. Their execution is subject to resource limits and the integration's timeout and error policies.

For the separate sequencer inclusion boundary, see [L2 and Sequencer Integrations](./l2-integrations/overview).

## Decentralized Builders

Assertion validation is not an Ethereum consensus rule. Protected operations need an unavoidable guard. The Credible RPC routes transactions to participating builders but cannot prevent inclusion by other builders.

The model relies on:

- **Authorized builders** validating applicable assertions and marking only blocks they build under that policy.
- **Marker integrity**: the marker is bound to the expected timestamp. The publication design relies on keeping the marker transaction private until the block header is signed and on validators not signing conflicting blocks for the same slot.
- **Registry administration** controlling builder authorization and any upgrade or emergency powers according to the deployed contracts.
- **Complete guard coverage**: protected entry points, internal paths, modules, and alternative execution routes must not bypass the intended gate.
- **Correct guard behavior** for credible blocks, noncredible blocks, Registry errors, fail-open conditions, and authorized bypasses.

A marker is an authorized attestation, not a cryptographic proof that assertions ran. A guard checks the marker rather than independently executing the rules. See [Architecture](./architecture-overview#decentralized-block-building).

## Authority Boundaries

| Role | Responsibility and risk |
| --- | --- |
| Protocol Manager | Controls assertion lifecycle for registered contracts. Compromise can permit restrictive rules or removal of protections, subject to authorization and timelocks. |
| Platform project members | Have the project permissions assigned to them. Platform access is separate from on-chain authority. |
| Protocol owners and upgrade admins | Control protocol changes and any guard installation, replacement, or bypass powers granted by the protocol. |
| Registry admins and guardians | Hold the administrative or emergency powers exposed by the deployed Registry. These must be reviewed separately from assertion-manager permissions. |
| Transaction submitters | Deliver transactions through the intended route. Submission access does not itself confer assertion-management authority. |

Assertion timelocks provide time to observe and respond to changes. They do not necessarily constrain separate guard, protocol-upgrade, Registry-admin, or guardian powers. Lost keys and compromised roles need a recovery design that remains executable under the active policy.

## Availability and Recovery

A guard can trade mandatory enforcement for availability by failing open after a configured gap in credible blocks. The block-gap condition opens when the gap is strictly greater than the threshold. During fail-open, protected operations can execute in blocks whose builders do not enforce assertions. Existing protocol checks remain in force, and participating builders can continue enforcing assertions.

Registry-error behavior is implementation-specific. The Safe guard includes fail-open handling for failed or malformed Registry responses; the generic contract guard can propagate a failed Registry read as a revert. Overrides and authorized bypasses can further change behavior.

A block-gap fallback does not necessarily resolve false positives. Builders may keep producing credible blocks while a restrictive assertion rejects a protocol's transactions. Rule updates, removal, or separately authorized recovery may be needed, with their own delays and permissions.

Each integration should define:

- How missing assertion artifacts, validation errors, timeouts, and overloaded workers affect inclusion.
- Which operations remain available during degraded service and which protections are relaxed.
- Who can change rules, guards, and recovery settings, and how those changes are monitored.
- How excluded transactions are reported, reconciled, and retried by submitters.
- How recovery works if a key is lost or an active rule rejects the recovery transaction.

## Evidence and Assurance

Review deployed contract paths, active policies, and administrative powers to assess coverage. Public code and registry state alone do not establish it. Project permissions control access to private invalidation data.

Assertion DA and local caches supply the bytecode used by enforcers. Integrations need defined behavior when artifacts cannot be prepared or validation cannot complete; hot-path validation should not depend on a live artifact fetch.

Local tests, release backtesting, and alerting provide evidence about rule behavior. End-to-end guard, routing, and recovery tests establish different parts of the integration. Audit reports apply to their stated revision and scope, not automatically to later changes or protocol-specific wiring.
